Growing Up to Learn

What reinforcement learning looks like when you raise it the way children are raised — with maps, drills, mess, teachers, and a body that starts small.

developmental-rl · numpy · 179 tests · every claim measured

scroll to begin

Reinforcement learning is usually told as a story about reward. An agent acts, the world scores it, and a policy slowly bends toward whatever pays. Imitation learning is the opposite story: copy the teacher and hope the world never drifts from the demonstrations.

Humans live between those stories. We take instruction — compressed experience handed down by people who practiced before us — and we cash it out through our own trial and error. We keep a model of the world in our heads and rehearse inside it. We drill small pieces before playing whole games. We practice with deliberate variation, because smooth repetition is a comfortable lie. And we do all of it inside a body that starts small and grows on a schedule that seems suspiciously convenient for learning.

This page reports five hypotheses about that middle ground, each turned into a falsifiable experiment on deliberately small systems — tabular and linear agents, gridworlds and a torque-limited pendulum — where every step is countable, budgets are matched to the step, and 20–40 seeds per condition make the statistics honest. Small worlds, so the mechanisms have nowhere to hide.

Protocol, in one line: IQM point estimates, 95% percentile-bootstrap CIs, two-sided Mann-Whitney with Holm correction, and identical evaluation for every condition. Full details in the methods section at the end.

the one idea you need

Every experiment here is tabular Q-learning: a table with one row per situation, one column per action, holding a running guess of long-run reward.

learning = leaking value backward

Reach the goal once and its value leaks one step back along the path. Arrows grow as the table converges. That's the whole engine.

why so small?

Because the hypotheses are about structure around the learner — models, drills, teachers, bodies. A small learner keeps the structure visible.

H1 · world models — chapter one

The Blindfold Test

Close your eyes in your own home and you can still find the fridge. Close your eyes in a stranger's home and you are lost immediately. Your motor neurons didn't change between those two sentences — your map did.

We raised an agent in a 13×11 apartment with 10% motor slip, letting it learn both a policy and a transition model of its home. Then we took its observations away entirely. Blindfolded, it navigates by dead reckoning: it imagines where each action should have taken it, tracks a belief over where it probably is, and asks its value table what to do from there. The test: same blindfold, familiar home versus stranger's home.

sighted · home A

With eyes open, the trained agent reaches the fridge in 99% of episodes. The spotlight is its observation.

lights out · home A

Blindfolded, pure dead reckoning drops to 21% — slip noise compounds, the belief drifts. Sound familiar? Add touch — a bump signal filtering the belief, a hand trailing the wall — and success returns to 96%, nearly sighted-level.

a stranger's home

Same agent, same blindfold, same touch — new floor plan. Success: 10%. Watch the ghost: the belief marches confidently through walls that aren't where it thinks they are.

the model is the difference

Sighted transfer to home B with home A's values also fails (0%). It was never about vision. It's the map.

Model-based learning is the other half of H1

Greedy success at the fridge, evaluated every 1k steps · 30 seeds · IQM with 95% bootstrap CI

The same experience, replayed through a learned model (Dyna-Q, 20 imagined updates per real step), reaches 90% success in 2,000 steps vs 24,938 model-free — a 12× gap (Holm p=3.1e-11). The map isn't just for blindfolds; it's a sample-efficiency engine.

H2 · microtasks — chapter two

Nobody Learns Soccer by Playing Soccer

Coaches don't start with full matches, and piano teachers don't start with the whole piece. They isolate: shoot from close, then dribble, then play. We asked whether the drill structure itself — with no shaping, no hints, no extra reward — is worth anything to a reinforcement learner.

Three agents got identical budgets of 60,000 steps on an 11×7 pitch where only goals are ever rewarded. One played full games from kickoff the whole time. One spent 20% of its budget spawning near the goal with the ball (shooting drill), 20% spawning in its own half (dribbling drill), then played full games. The only thing the curriculum changes is where episodes start.

whole games

From kickoff, a goal is a long corridor of luck away. The value table waits most of the run for its first win.

drills first

Near the goal, wins are cheap — value pools around scoring. The dribble drill then connects midfield to that pool. Full games inherit a table that already knows how stories end.

the same steps, spent better

Time to 90% success: drilled 15,375 steps vs whole-game 38,875 (p=1.1e-9). Fixed-spawn drills land between — variety inside the drill matters too.

Reverse curriculum over start states

Full-game success from kickoff, 20 greedy evals / 2k steps · 30 seeds · IQM ± 95% CI

Same environment, same reward, same total steps. The drilled agents' curves lift off during the drill phases (shaded) and never look back. Four whole-game seeds never reached 90% at all.

H3 · variation — chapter three

Practice That Feels Worse and Works Better

In 1979, Shea and Morgan split learners into two groups: one drilled each movement pattern in a block, one practiced them shuffled. Blocked practice looked better during training — and lost on retention and transfer. Motor-learning calls it the contextual-interference effect. We reproduced it in a learner small enough to explain.

Three short piano passages share a library of six three-note motifs; each passage has one exception where its fingering breaks the pattern. A linear learner practices either blocked (all of passage A, then B, then C) or interleaved (a random passage each episode). Same total practice. The interference lives exactly where the skill does: in the shared motif weights.

blocked · drill one passage interleaved · shuffle passages
watch the fool's progress

The blocked lane looks immaculate: every rep on the same passage, score climbing. Acquisition IQM 0.80 vs 0.77 — blocked genuinely wins the practice room (p=.006).

then comes the recital

Test everything. The passage drilled first has decayed under two passages of shared-weight drift: retention on A — blocked 0.73, interleaved 0.92 (p=7e-10).

and a new piece

On a never-practiced passage built from the same motifs, interleaved transfers 0.75 vs 0.64 (p=.006). Variation forced the weights to encode the motifs, not the last passage rehearsed.

The crossover, in silico

Left: score on the currently-practiced passage during training. Right: test with learning off · 40 seeds

Blocked's one honest win: the passage it drilled last (retention on C: 0.99). Recency is real; it's just not learning. The crossover pattern — train worse, test better — is the Shea-Morgan signature, reproduced by shared-parameter interference alone.

H4 · teachers — chapter four

The One Time I Reached the Mountain

No pianist learns from scratch. Teachers hand down compressed trajectories — practice this passage, this way — distilled from their teachers, and theirs. The knowledge crosses generations through a narrow channel: not brains, stories. We built the smallest version of that channel we could measure.

A gridworld hides a big reward far to the right, guarded by candy — small terminal rewards near the start that end episodes early and teach agents to stop looking. Each agent lives 15,000 steps with plasticity that decays as it ages: learning rate and exploration halve every 5,000 steps. Old agents remember their three best episodes ever — but can no longer act on them. At death, those episodes become advice: at most 100 state-action pairs, seeded optimistically into a fresh, fully plastic student.

generation 1

Lives, gorges on candy, stumbles onto the mountain once late in life — too rigid to consolidate it. Greedy performance at death: candy (0.3). Best memory: the mountain (10).

the bottleneck

Its advice is not its brain. It's the skeleton of its best days — the one lucky deep run — passed to a student plastic enough to finish the thought.

generation 5

The lineage ratchets: 0.3 → 1.25 → 4.4 → 7.5 → 8.75 IQM. 70% of lineages end at the mountain. Every control — weight-copy, one long life, slow decay, no inheritance — stays at candy forever, on every seed.

why copying fails

The weight-copy makes the student inherit the whole trap-shaped value table — consolidated mediocrity. The bottleneck transmits only the peak. Losing almost everything is the feature.

Five generations, five conditions

Greedy return at each generation's end · 30 seeds · IQM ± 95% CI · all four comparisons Holm p=.014

The gap between what long-lived agents remember (best episode ≈ mountain) and what they can do (candy) stays at ~3 points for 75,000 steps — they know the way and cannot walk it. Distillation converts that gap into the next generation's head start; by generation 5 it has closed to 0.7.

H5 · morphology — chapter five

Small Bodies Fall Cheaply

Toddlers fall about seventeen times an hour and shrug it off — small bodies carry little energy into the ground. By the square-cube law they are also relatively strong: strength scales with s², weight with s³. Falling is how you learn to stand, and evolution priced falling at its cheapest exactly when you need thousands of them.

Our learner balances a torque-limited pendulum whose scale s sets everything honestly: length s, mass 15s³, strength 40s², and fall damage (s)⁴ — impact energy. Six conditions, 120,000 steps each, all evaluated on the adult body. Some train adult-sized from day one. Some start at half size and grow — on a schedule, or adaptively, growing only when the current body stops falling.

born adult

Every early mistake lands at full mass and full height. Damage meter: spinning. It does learn — at a price of 880 damage units to reach competence.

grow when ready

The adaptive grower masters each body before earning the next. Falls happen — at toddler prices. Damage to the same adult competence: 62. Fourteen times cheaper, and no slower in steps (p=.30).

skills survive growth

The policy transfers across nearby bodies as the dynamics shift smoothly under it — competence rides the growth curve up to a body it has barely trained on.

Damage accumulated on the way to adult competence

Cumulative fall damage vs training step · 20 seeds · IQM ± 95% CI · damage = (size)⁴ per fall

Growing bodies buy competence with an order of magnitude less damage (Holm p ≤ 2.1e-6). In animals, damage is death, injury, and metabolic cost — the currency evolution actually optimizes.

What didn't survive contact with the data

Two of our own sub-claims failed their tests. They stay in the paper.

refuted · balance-firstWe predicted "learn to balance, then walk," like infants. Backwards, here: walking-from-the-start reached competence faster (Holm p=3e-4). Weight-shifting practice visits the states balancing needs; a balance-only phase starves the walking half of the table. Infant staging may serve growing bodies and safety — not the value function.
null · gradualismGrowing in small increments wasn't reliably better than one jump from half-size to adult (p=.11, point estimate reversed). The savings come from being small while incompetent — not from the smoothness of the schedule.

synthesis

Five Curves, One Lesson

Every mechanism that worked, worked the same way: it changed which experience the learner got, not how the learner updates. The map replayed experience. Drills repositioned it. Variation shuffled it. Teachers curated it across lifetimes. Small bodies discounted its price.

That is what "between imitation and evaluation" means operationally. Pure evaluation wastes lifetimes rediscovering what the last lifetime knew; pure imitation caps the student at the teacher. The productive middle is a narrow, curated channel of experience — a curriculum, an anecdote, a drill, a start state — feeding a learner that remains free to disagree.
